Stories & Essays

The pleasure of wondering where an idea might lead.

For much of my life, writing has been one of my favorite ways of exploring the world and my relationship with it.

Sometimes I begin with a question. Sometimes with a character, an observation, a conversation, or an idea that seems sufficiently ridiculous to be worth exploring.

I rarely know exactly where a piece of writing will take me when I begin. That uncertainty is often part of the pleasure of writing it.

The writings gathered here span nearly four decades, different countries, imagined worlds, personal experiences, and changing ways of understanding life.

Some are serious. Some are playful. Some are both. And some began with questions that I am still asking today.

The Freedom to Imagine

Serious questions do not always require serious stories.

One of the pleasures of fiction is that it allows me to create circumstances that do not necessarily exist, introduce people who have never lived, and explore questions that might otherwise be difficult to ask.

An imaginary conversation can reveal something about a real relationship. An absurd situation can expose the absurdity of something we ordinarily consider perfectly reasonable.

Humor can sometimes help us notice things that seriousness prevents us from seeing.

And sometimes a story is simply enjoyable because it is a story. It need not justify its existence by providing a lesson.

From the Notebooks

Conversations preserved on paper.

Behind many of my published writings are years of notebooks, journals, handwritten manuscripts, typed pages, and unfinished ideas.

For many years, particularly while living abroad, writing in my notebooks was an important part of my daily life.

They contain observations, conversations, philosophical explorations, personal experiences, drawings, and ideas that sometimes developed into stories and sometimes remained simply ideas.

Revisiting these notebooks decades later has become another kind of discovery. I occasionally find something I had completely forgotten writing. At other times, I recognize the beginnings of questions that have accompanied me throughout much of my life.

A Conversation Across Time

There is something curious about reading one's own writing nearly forty years later.

The younger writer did not know the experiences that lay ahead of him. He had not yet encountered many of the people, places, relationships, and circumstances that would become part of his life.

Yet I sometimes discover that he was asking questions that remain interesting to me today.

I do not want to replace all of his questions with my present answers. Nor do I want to assume that growing older has necessarily made every aspect of my understanding more interesting.

There are things the younger writer noticed that the older writer might otherwise have forgotten to notice.

Writing Is Thinking

I do not always write because I have something to say. Sometimes I write because I want to discover what I have to say.

An idea that seems perfectly clear in my mind can become considerably less clear when I attempt to express it in words.

Writing gives me an opportunity to examine that idea, recognize its assumptions, discover relationships, and sometimes reconsider it entirely.

This relationship between language and understanding has also become an important part of my life as an educator.

The Ten Stairs of Critical Thinking

The Ten Stairs represent a framework I have developed for exploring how we move from recognizing something toward increasingly complex forms of understanding.

1. Identify

Recognize what is there.

2. Define

Establish what it means.

3. Illustrate

Make understanding visible through examples and representations.

4. Describe

Explore characteristics, qualities, and details.

5. Narrate

Explore events, experiences, and their development.

6. Explain

Explore how and why something happens or exists.

7. Analyze

Examine relationships, structures, and patterns.

8. Evaluate

Consider evidence, significance, and value.

9. Teach

Develop and communicate understanding through sharing it with others.

10. Reflect

Reconsider what has been learned and discover possibilities for further learning.

Although these Stairs provide a structure for developing understanding, curiosity remains an important reason for climbing them.

Reflection does not necessarily bring the journey to an end. It can introduce another question, another observation, and another opportunity to begin.
