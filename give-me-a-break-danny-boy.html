Fiction

Give Me a Break, Danny Boy

By Bradley Arnold

Movement 1 – Questions

Danny arrived ten minutes early.

He always did. He hated waiting at home.

The library was quiet. It asked him fewer questions than other places.

He pushed open the heavy wooden door and found me exactly where he expected.

Surrounded by books. Writing in a notebook.

“You read too much.”

I looked up.

“Good morning to you too.”

“It’s true.”

He dropped into the chair across from me.

“I’ve never seen anyone read as much as you.”

“I’ve never seen anyone ask as many questions as you.”

Danny smiled. “I don’t ask that many.”

“You asked seventeen yesterday.”

“I counted.”

His eyes widened.

“You counted?”

“No.”

“I made that up.”

Danny put his hand in front of his mouth and giggled. He then spun gently in his chair.

I pretended to read.

Eventually…he broke the silence. “I want to ask you something.”

“I’ve been waiting.”

“Do teachers know everything?”

I looked around the library. “I certainly hope not.”

Danny frowned. “Why?”

“Well, if they already knew everything…” I closed the book. “…what would be left to learn?”

He considered that. “I thought teachers were supposed to know the answers.”

“Not all of them.”

“That doesn’t make sense. Then how do they become teachers?”

“They keep asking questions. Those new questions usually help them find the answer they are looking for.”

Danny looked suspicious. “That doesn’t sound right.”

“It usually doesn’t.”

He folded his arms. “I think you’re trying to trick me.”

“I think you’re giving me too much credit.”

Danny pointed toward the shelves. “Have you read all these books?”

“No.”

“Half?”

“No.”

“A hundred?”

I shrugged. “Maybe.”

His eyes grew wide. “A hundred books?”

“Probably.”

“So…” He leaned forward. “…you know a whole lot.”

“I know a little.”

“From a hundred books?”

“Yes.”

“Then what’s in the rest of them?”

I shrugged. “Things I don’t know yet.”

Danny sat, a pensive look on his face. That answer seemed to bother him.

Finally he said, “I don’t like not knowing.”

“Neither do I.”

“You don’t?”

“No.”

“Then what do you do?”

I thought for a moment. “I keep wondering.”

“That doesn’t make it better.”

“No.”

“Then why do it?”

“Because every now and then…” I raised my eyebrows and looked up, “…wondering leads to a whole new place.”

Danny looked down at the floor. “Does it always?”

“No.”

“That doesn’t sound fair.”

“It isn’t.”

Danny sighed dramatically. “Give me a break.”

I laughed. “I know.”

Danny shook his head. “Grown-ups are weird.”

“I’ve noticed.”

He stood and wandered toward the nearest bookshelf. Running one finger across the rows of books. “So…” he called over his shoulder, “…if teachers don’t know everything…” He paused. “…who does?”

The question drifted through the library.

I looked out the window for a moment before answering. “I’ve never met that person.”

“You mean…” Danny turned around carefully. “…nobody knows everything?”

“I don’t think so.”

He looked disappointed. For five whole seconds.

Then his lips spread across his face. “Good.”

“Why good?”

“That means I can keep asking questions.”

“I was hoping you’d say that.”

Danny nodded proudly. “I knew it.”

Movement 2 – Give Me a Break

Danny pulled another chair away from the table and straddled it backwards.

He always sat that way when he was preparing an argument.

“I still don’t believe you.”

“I can tell.”

“You say teachers don’t know everything.”

“That’s right.”

“But yesterday Sarah asked you what the tallest mountain in the world was.”

“I remember.”

“And you knew.”

“I did.”

“And Tommy asked why the moon changes shape.”

“I answered.”

“And Jennifer asked what a noun was.”

“I answered that too.”

“So…” Danny folded his arms triumphantly. “…you know everything.”

I curled my lip over and nodded my head. “That’s impressive logic.”

“I know.”

“No. You think you know.”

Danny rolled his eyes. “You’re doing that teacher thing again.”

“What teacher thing?”

“Changing the words.”

I leaned back in my chair. “You caught me.”

“I usually do.”

“I’ve noticed.”

Danny looked far too pleased with himself.

“So admit it.” He looked hard at me. “You know everything.”

“I’ll admit something.”

He waited.

“I know more now than I did when I was your age.”

Danny nodded. “Exactly.”

“And less than I’ll know when I’m eighty.”

He frowned. “How do you know that?”

“Because I’ll keep asking questions.”

“But what if you don’t?”

“Then I’ll stop being the kind of teacher I want to be.”

Danny stared at me. “You really mean that?”

“I do.”

“I thought teachers were supposed to teach.”

“They are.”

“They are not supposed to learn. Learning is for students.”

“They have to do both.”

“Why?”

I shrugged. “If I stop learning, what kind of example am I giving you?”

Danny looked down at the tabletop. “My dad says when you become a grown-up…” He traced little circles with one finger. “…you already know what you’re going to know.”

“My dad used to say things like that too.”

“Were your parents wrong?”

I looked at him thoughtfully. “Sometimes.”

Danny’s eyes grew enormous.

“You can say that and not be afraid?”

“I just did.”

He whispered, “I don’t think my mom would like you saying that.”

“I don’t think my mom would have either.”

For a moment, we shared a laugh.

“So…” Danny turned serious again. “…who taught the first teacher?”

I blinked. “I have absolutely no idea.”

“I knew it!” He threw his arms up as if in celebration.

“No. You wondered it.”

“Same difference.”

“Not even close.”

He leaned forward across the table. “Ok… What if I ask you something you don’t know?”

“Go ahead.”

He thought. Thought harder.

Then opened his mouth. “What color were the first dinosaurs?”

“I don’t know.”

“Ha!” He pointed at me as though he’d just won a championship. “You admitted it.”

“I certainly did.”

“You weren’t embarrassed.”

“Should I have been?”

“I would have.”

“Why?”

“Because…” Danny hesitated. “…people might think I was dumb.”

I realized we weren’t talking about dinosaurs anymore.

“Danny…” I looked at him carefully. “Can I share a secret with you?”

He nodded.

“The smartest people I’ve ever met said…” I stopped and made sure our eyes met. “…‘I don’t know’ all the time.”

He frowned. “Really?”

“Nearly every day.”

“Then how did they become smart?”

“They kept asking questions.”

Danny sat still. His feet no longer swung beneath the chair. He wasn’t trying to win now.

He was thinking.

“So,” he looked up, “I don’t know’ isn’t the end.”

“No.” I smiled. “It’s usually the beginning.”

Danny nodded. Then, under his breath, he muttered, “Give me a break.”

“What?”

“If that’s true…” He looked up, “then I’ve got a whole lot of beginnings left.”

“So do I.”

Movement 3 – Treasure

Danny slid off the chair and wandered through the children’s section. He wasn’t looking for any book. He was looking for something interesting.

Every so often he would stop. Pull a book from the shelf. Look at the cover. Shake his head. Put it back.

I watched him from across the room.

He reminded me of a person from my past. It took me a moment to recall who… Me.

Danny bent over excitedly.

I asked, “What did you find?”

He held up a round object between two fingers. A shiny marble. Dark blue with thin white streaks twisting through the middle.

“It was under the bookshelf.” He smiled proudly. “I found treasure.”

“I think you did.”

He rolled the marble across his palm. “I wonder who lost it.”

“I don’t know.”

“You say that a lot.”

“I’ve been trying to improve.”

Danny laughed. He slipped the marble into his pocket.

“I get to keep it.”

“If nobody claims it.”

“I hope nobody does.”

“So do I.”

We walked toward the library window. Outside, children were chasing one another across the playground.

Danny watched them for a while. “They’re not learning anything.”

I looked at him. “No?”

“They’re just playing.”

“What if playing is one way of learning?”

Danny frowned. “How?”

“They’re learning who runs faster. They’re learning what happens when there is a fall. They’re learning when to laugh. They’re learning to help when another one of them gets hurt. They’re learning how to take turns. They’re learning how to lose. They’re learning how to make up after an argument.”

I shrugged. “Looks like a pretty busy classroom to me.”

Danny stared out the window. “I never thought about that.”

“I know.”

“So…” He looked back at me. “…am I learning right now?”

“What do you think?”

He seemed to struggle for a moment. “I don’t know.”

For a while we stood together. Watching the playground. Watching the library. Watching people come and go carrying books much heavier than they were.

Finally Danny reached into his pocket and pulled out the marble again.

He held it toward me. “You can have it.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“You found it.”

“But you’re the teacher.”

“I know.”

“So you should have it.”

I shook my head. “I think teachers collect different kinds of treasure.”

Danny looked puzzled. “What kind?”

I thought for a moment. Then pointed toward him.

“You.”

He blinked. “Me?”

“You.”

“But I’m not a treasure.”

“That’s where you’re wrong.”

Danny looked down at the marble. Then back at me. “You really believe that?”

“I do.”

“Even when I ask too many questions?”

“Especially then.”

Danny said nothing. Much longer than normal.

Finally he slipped the marble back into his pocket.

“I think…” he said carefully, “…if people are treasures, then books are maps.”

I stared at him.

“Why are you looking at me like that?”

I laughed. “Because I wish I had thought of that.”

“I guess I taught you something.”

“You certainly did.”

He folded his arms with great satisfaction. “See?”

“Teachers don’t know everything.”

“No.”

I nodded. “That’s why we need our students.”

Movement 4 – The Library

Danny stopped in front of the card catalog. He looked at drawer after drawer of tiny wooden handles.

“There are a million books in here.”

“Not quite.”

“A hundred thousand?”

“I’ve never counted.”

He looked confused. “You don’t know?”

“I’ve never counted.”

Danny laughed. “I knew you were going to say that.”

He pulled open one of the drawers.

Hundreds of little cards. Names. Numbers. Titles. Each one pointing to another place.

“It looks like a treasure map.”

“It does.”

“But where’s the treasure?”

I smiled. “That’s the fun part.”

“You don’t know?”

“I don’t know which treasure you’ll find.”

“So…” Danny slid the drawer shut. “…how do you know where to look?”

“You begin with the question.”

“And then?”

“You follow it.”

“What if it changes?”

“It usually does.”

Danny frowned. “Questions change?”

“Good ones. And you keep following them.”

We walked between the shelves.

“So…” Danny ran his fingers across rows of books. “…when you come to the library, you don’t already know what you’ll learn?”

“No.”

“Then why come?”

“Well, maybe every now and then…” I looked around the room. “…I leave with a better question than the one I brought in.”

Danny stopped walking. “I thought you came here for answers.”

“I used to.”

“What changed?”

“I kept discovering that answers have a funny habit.”

Danny scratched his head. “What?”

“They create new questions.”

Danny nodded thoughtfully. “I guess that’s true.”

“When I found the marble… I wanted to know who lost it.”

“And now?”

“Now I wonder where it came from.”

“And after that?”

He scratched his head and said. “I’ll probably wonder where marbles came from.”

“And after that?”

He threw his hands in the air. “Give me a break!”

We both laughed loud enough that the librarian looked over her glasses at us.

Danny lowered his voice immediately. “Sorry.”

The librarian smiled. “It’s all right.”

Then she looked at Danny. “I like curious people.”

Danny looked at me. “I thought librarians liked quiet people.”

“They do.” She returned to her work. “But curiosity is worth a little noise.”

Danny beamed all the way to the history section.

“You planned that.”

“I’ve never met her before.”

“I don’t believe you.”

“You don’t have to.”

He suddenly stopped in front of a thick encyclopedia. “Can I ask you one more question?”

“You just did.”

He rolled his eyes. “You know what I mean.”

“I do.”

“Will there ever be a day… when I don’t have any more questions?”

I didn’t answer immediately. Instead, I pulled a large atlas from the shelf and opened it across a nearby table.

The world spread out before us. Oceans. Mountains. Rivers. Countries. Thousands of places neither of us had ever seen.

I pointed to the map. “What do you notice?”

Danny looked carefully. “It’s big.”

“It is. What else?”

“There are places I’ve never heard of.”

“The same for me.”

He looked at me. “You?”

“Lots of places.”

He stared at the map a little longer. Then a certain confidence seemed to come to him.

“I don’t think I’m ever going to run out of questions.”

“No, I don’t think you will, Danny. I don’t think the world is going to let you.”

Danny closed the atlas slowly. “I like that.”

“What?”

“That the world is bigger than me.”

I nodded. “So do I.”

He looked around the library one last time.

“I thought this place had all the answers.”

“What do you think now?”

“I think,” he said confidently, “it’s full of better questions.”

Movement 5 – Walking Home

When we stepped outside, the afternoon sun had settled low enough to cast long shadows across the sidewalk.

Danny looked down. “My shadow’s taller than I am.”

“For another hour.”

“Then what?”

“The sun keeps moving.”

Danny nodded. “I guess everything keeps moving.”

We walked along.

Danny kicked at a small pebble. Watched it skip ahead of them. Every few steps he caught up to it and kicked it again.

Finally he said, “I want to tell you something.”

“Please do.”

“I don’t think I came here to learn the answer to my question.”

“No?”

“I think…” He searched for the words. “…I came here because I wanted you to know what it was.”

I smiled. “And now?”

He looked ahead. “I kind of like that you didn’t.”

We walked another block before Danny spoke again.

“You know…” he said, “…I used to think teachers were like encyclopedias.”

“And now?”

“I think they’re more like flashlights.”

I looked over. “Flashlights?”

“They don’t show you what’s everywhere… They just help you see what’s in front of you.”

For a long moment I said nothing.

Danny noticed. “Was that a good answer?”

“It was a beautiful answer.”

“I’ve been practicing.” He seemed proud of himself.

As we reached the corner where we’d go our separate ways, Danny reached into his pocket and took out the blue marble.

He held it up toward the afternoon sun.

The white streaks inside it caught the light and suddenly seemed brighter than before.

“It looks different.”

“It is different.”

He frowned. “I don’t think so. It hasn’t changed.”

“Maybe you have.”

Danny looked at the marble again. He slipped it back into his pocket. “Can I ask you one last question?”

“You’ve earned one.”

“Only one?”

“Today.”

He thought carefully. Longer than usual.

Then he looked up. “When I grow up… do I have to stop wondering?”

“I certainly hope not.”

“Why?”

“Because the day you stop wondering… the world starts becoming smaller.”

Danny looked around. The trees. The houses. The people walking past. The clouds drifting slowly overhead.

“I don’t think I want a smaller world.”

“Neither do I.”

Danny started toward home. After taking a couple steps he turned around. “Oh…”

“What?”

“I never did ask the question I came here to ask.”

“No.”

“You didn’t.”

“Do you want to know what it was?”

I shook my head. “No.”

He looked puzzled. “Why not?”

I smiled. “I think you’ll enjoy discovering it more than hearing the answer.”

Danny stared at me for a moment. Then a slow grin spread across his face.

“You really are weird.”

“I’ve been told.”

He laughed and waved. Then disappeared around the corner.

I stood there for another minute. Watching the empty sidewalk. Thinking about marbles. Libraries. Questions. Teachers. Children. And the curious way together they teach people about living.

It’s true. Teachers really don’t know everything.

Often…their students teach them the most important lessons of all.
