The Red Key

Reflections, Interruptions, and Becoming

How much of who we are today began in moments we barely remember?

When I entered recovery, I gradually discovered that alcohol had been a symptom of something much deeper: codependency.

The Red Key is an exploration of how I became the person who entered recovery. It returns to childhood memories, relationships, adventures, accomplishments, disappointments, and discoveries with questions I had not previously known to ask.

It is a book about understanding the past, experiencing the present, and discovering the possibilities of becoming.

The Meaning of the Red Key

The title comes from a childhood memory involving a typewriter and a red key I had been told not to press.

It was a seemingly small incident. Yet something about that experience remained with me throughout my life.

Returning to that memory many decades later, I began to recognize connections between curiosity, disobedience, shame, remorse, acceptance, and the desire to be loved.

The red key became a way of exploring something much larger than the incident itself: the ways in which our experiences gradually shape our understanding of who we are and who we believe we need to be.

Sometimes the smallest memories open the largest doors.

Looking Back to Understand

Recognizing my codependency required more than identifying certain behaviors. It required reconsidering the beliefs, fears, desires, and expectations that had helped shape those behaviors.

Many of the patterns I began to recognize in recovery had been present long before alcohol became part of my life.

My desire to be accepted, to be needed, to accomplish something meaningful, and to become someone worthy of love had influenced decisions and relationships in ways I had rarely understood.

Remembering

Returning to the experiences themselves, including those that once seemed ordinary or insignificant.

Recognizing

Discovering connections between experiences, relationships, beliefs, and recurring patterns of behavior.

Becoming

Learning that understanding the past can create opportunities to experience the present differently.

The purpose of looking backward is not to assign blame or to explain away everything I have done.

It is to understand myself more fully and to become more willing to participate honestly in my own life.

A Life Worth Exploring

My life has been filled with curiosity, imagination, travel, relationships, writing, teaching, and discovery.

It has taken me from childhood experiences in the United States to years of living in Cameroon, Thailand, and Sudan. It has brought me into contact with different cultures, languages, beliefs, and ways of understanding the world.

Along the way there have been moments of extraordinary beauty, unexpected kindness, laughter, adventure, loneliness, confusion, and loss.

Recognizing my codependency does not diminish the richness of those experiences. Nor does it mean that everything I valued, pursued, or accomplished was somehow less meaningful.

Part of recovery has been learning to appreciate those experiences without needing them to establish my worth.

The Red Key is therefore as much an exploration of life as it is an exploration of recovery.

Why I Wrote This Book

There is a difference between remembering an experience and understanding it.

Many of the memories in this book had accompanied me for decades. I had told some of the stories repeatedly. Others had found their way into journals, essays, conversations, and earlier writings.

What changed was not necessarily the memories. What changed were the questions I began asking about them.

Writing allowed me to interrupt familiar explanations of who I was and why I had lived as I had. It gave me opportunities to reconsider assumptions that had become so familiar that I rarely recognized them as assumptions.

I began to discover connections between experiences that had once seemed unrelated, and to recognize patterns that had been difficult for me to see while I was living through them.

I am not writing from the perspective of someone who has finally figured everything out.

I am writing as someone who is still discovering, still questioning, and still becoming.

Rather than simply recounting what happened, the book considers how those experiences influenced the ways I understood myself, related to others, and participated in life.

A Companion Journey

When Joy Says Begin

While The Red Key explores how I became the person who entered recovery, When Joy Says Begin explores the discoveries I began making through recovery itself.

One looks backward to understand the development of codependency. The other looks forward through the practice of recovery and the possibilities of living.

Both books are ultimately concerned with life, living, and joy.
