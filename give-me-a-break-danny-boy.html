Philosophical Fiction

Give Me a Break, Danny Boy

By Bradley Arnold

Opening movement of a five-movement story

Movement 1 – Questions

Danny arrived ten minutes early.

He always did. He hated waiting at home.

The library was quiet. It asked him fewer questions than other places.

He pushed open the heavy wooden door and found me exactly where he expected.

Surrounded by books. Writing in a notebook.

“You read too much.”

I looked up.

“Good morning to you too.”

“It’s true.”

He dropped into the chair across from me.

“I’ve never seen anyone read as much as you.”

“I’ve never seen anyone ask as many questions as you.”

Danny smiled. “I don’t ask that many.”

“You asked seventeen yesterday.”

“I counted.”

His eyes widened.

“You counted?”

“No.”

“I made that up.”

Danny put his hand in front of his mouth and giggled. He then spun gently in his chair.

I pretended to read.

Eventually…he broke the silence. “I want to ask you something.”

“I’ve been waiting.”

“Do teachers know everything?”

I looked around the library. “I certainly hope not.”

Danny frowned. “Why?”

“Well, if they already knew everything…” I closed the book. “…what would be left to learn?”

He considered that. “I thought teachers were supposed to know the answers.”

“Not all of them.”

“That doesn’t make sense. Then how do they become teachers?”

“They keep asking questions. Those new questions usually help them find the answer they are looking for.”

Danny looked suspicious. “That doesn’t sound right.”

“It usually doesn’t.”

He folded his arms. “I think you’re trying to trick me.”

“I think you’re giving me too much credit.”

Danny pointed toward the shelves. “Have you read all these books?”

“No.”

“Half?”

“No.”

“A hundred?”

I shrugged. “Maybe.”

His eyes grew wide. “A hundred books?”

“Probably.”

“So…” He leaned forward. “…you know a whole lot.”

“I know a little.”

“From a hundred books?”

“Yes.”

“Then what’s in the rest of them?”

I shrugged. “Things I don’t know yet.”

Danny sat, a pensive look on his face. That answer seemed to bother him.

Finally he said, “I don’t like not knowing.”

“Neither do I.”

“You don’t?”

“No.”

“Then what do you do?”

I thought for a moment. “I keep wondering.”

“That doesn’t make it better.”

“No.”

“Then why do it?”

“Because every now and then…” I raised my eyebrows and looked up, “…wondering leads to a whole new place.”

Danny looked down at the floor. “Does it always?”

“No.”

“That doesn’t sound fair.”

“It isn’t.”

Danny sighed dramatically. “Give me a break.”

I laughed. “I know.”

Danny shook his head. “Grown-ups are weird.”

“I’ve noticed.”

He stood and wandered toward the nearest bookshelf. Running one finger across the rows of books. “So…” he called over his shoulder, “…if teachers don’t know everything…” He paused. “…who does?”

The question drifted through the library.

I looked out the window for a moment before answering. “I’ve never met that person.”

“You mean…” Danny turned around carefully. “…nobody knows everything?”

“I don’t think so.”

He looked disappointed. For five whole seconds.

Then his lips spread across his face. “Good.”

“Why good?”

“That means I can keep asking questions.”

“I was hoping you’d say that.”

Danny nodded proudly. “I knew it.”

Behind the Writing

Danny comes to the library with a question. The conversation turns on what happens when a teacher is willing to say “I don’t know,” and a student discovers that this leaves him room to keep asking.
