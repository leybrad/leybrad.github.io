A Thing Like No Other Thing

The imaginative world of a young writer.

What happens when an idea is allowed to wander wherever it wishes?

Written in 1987, these pieces explore the curious, humorous, philosophical, and occasionally absurd experience of being human.

They belong to a period of my life when writing was becoming an adventure in itself, a means of following ideas, entertaining possibilities, and discovering unexpected questions.

Nearly forty years later, they offer an opportunity to encounter that younger writer and the imaginative world he was creating.

1987: A World of Possibilities

In 1987, much of the life that I would eventually write about still lay ahead of me.

My experiences in Cameroon, Thailand, and Sudan, my years as an educator, and many of the relationships and discoveries that would influence my later writings were still in the future.

Yet I was already fascinated by questions about language, perception, identity, existence, and the peculiar ways in which human beings attempt to understand their world.

Writing gave me a place to explore those questions.

I could invent characters, create situations, follow unusual arguments, entertain rather absurd possibilities, and discover what happened when seemingly unrelated ideas came together.

These writings belong to that period of exploration, and to the particular person who was experiencing it.

The Freedom to Play with Ideas

Ideas need not always be useful.

They need not always lead to a conclusion, solve a problem, or provide an explanation.

Sometimes an idea is worth exploring simply because it is interesting.

An apparently ridiculous question may lead somewhere unexpectedly interesting. A fictional character may develop a perspective that challenges the person who created him. An ordinary observation may become the beginning of an extraordinary adventure.

Humor and philosophical inquiry need not be separate pursuits. Sometimes laughter allows us to notice things that our seriousness prevents us from seeing.

Much of the pleasure of these early writings lies in that freedom to explore without knowing precisely where the exploration will lead.

Discovering Through Writing

Writing was becoming much more than a means of communicating something I already understood.

It was becoming a way of thinking.

An idea that seemed relatively simple before I began writing could become considerably more complicated once I attempted to put it into words.

One sentence suggested another. A question introduced another question. A character or situation opened possibilities that had not occurred to me when I began.

Sometimes I discovered that I was writing about something quite different from what I originally thought I was writing about.

The process itself was a source of enjoyment.

That relationship between writing and discovery has remained an important part of my creative life.

Meeting Myself Nearly Forty Years Later

There is something curious about reading one's own writing nearly forty years later.

I recognize the person who wrote these pieces, although I sometimes find myself surprised by him.

He did not know the life that lay ahead of him. He could not have anticipated many of the experiences that would eventually influence his understanding of life.

Nevertheless, he had his own experiences, observations, fascinations, and questions.

I do not wish to introduce him merely as an earlier and less experienced version of myself.

His curiosity was genuine. His imagination was alive. His questions were worth asking.

Publishing these writings provides an opportunity for others to encounter him, and perhaps to discover something interesting in the world he was exploring.

Inside the Book

A Thing Like No Other Thing brings together early literary and philosophical explorations from a particular period of my life.

The collection moves between imagination, humor, reflection, and intellectual inquiry.

Readers may encounter questions that seem familiar, situations that seem unfamiliar, and ideas that resist comfortable classification.

The pieces can be approached individually, allowing each to become its own little adventure.

Before They Became a Book

For decades, these writings belonged to a much larger collection of notebooks, manuscripts, and creative explorations from different periods of my life.

Returning to early manuscripts is an experience quite different from reading a newly published book.

The pages belong not only to the history of a particular piece of writing, but also to the history of the person who created it.

They preserve something of the questions, interests, and creative energy of another time.
